How does an airplane fly?

Flying is really two problems at once: getting an object heavier than air up into the sky and keeping it there, and then steering it once it is up. Four forces are at work the whole time an airplane is flying.

The four forces

Weight is the pull of gravity, always pointing straight down toward the centre of the Earth. It depends on everything the plane carries: the aircraft itself, its fuel, and its passengers and cargo. During a flight the weight constantly changes as fuel is burned, so the pilot has to keep adjusting the controls to keep the plane balanced, or “trimmed.”

Lift is the force that fights back against weight. It is generated by the motion of the airplane through the air, and it points upward, at right angles to the direction the plane is travelling. Most of the lift comes from the wings, and it all acts through a single point called the centre of pressure.

Drag is air’s resistance to the plane moving through it. It points backwards, directly against the direction of flight. Like lift, it depends on the aircraft’s shape, how “sticky” the air is, and how fast the plane is going.

Thrust is the forward push from the engines, and its job is simply to overcome drag. Jet engines do this by blasting hot gas out the back, which pushes the plane forward: an action-and-reaction effect explained by Newton’s Third Law of Motion.

Bernoulli’s principle: how a wing makes lift

The secret of lift is Bernoulli’s principle, a discovery from the 1700s about moving fluids. In simple terms: where air flows faster, its pressure is lower; where air flows slower, its pressure is higher.

p + ½ρV2 = constant

A wing exploits this because of its shape, called an airfoil. Air flowing over the curved upper surface speeds up compared with the air flowing underneath. Because of Bernoulli’s principle, that faster air above the wing sits at lower pressure, while the slower air below stays at higher pressure.

The result is a pressure difference: higher pressure underneath pushing up, lower pressure on top pulling up, and the net effect is lift. Engineers calculate the pressure at every point along the wing’s surface, then add up that surface pressure to find the total aerodynamic force on the wing, which can be split into lift and drag.

The wing does not simply “push down on air” to float. It shapes the airflow so that the air itself creates lower pressure on top and higher pressure below.

A common misconception: the engines do not lift the plane

This surprises most people, but the engine’s job is only to overcome drag, not to lift the airplane. It is the wings that do the lifting. That is why gliders can fly with no engines at all, why a paper airplane flies, and why the Space Shuttle glided back to Earth: once something is moving fast enough, the wings generate lift.

How the pilot steers

If the four forces are perfectly balanced, the plane cruises at a constant speed; if one force is stronger, the plane accelerates in that direction. Turning and steering are done with moveable surfaces on the wings and tail that change the local lift. They control the aircraft in roll (tilting the wings), pitch (nose up or down), and yaw (nose left or right).

Putting it all together

The reason the airplane works at all is that it is designed as one connected system. The earliest successful airplane builders succeeded where others failed because they treated the airplane as an integrated whole, balancing aerodynamics, propulsion, structures, and control within a single framework.

In one sentence: the engines pull the plane forward fast enough that its curved wings create lower pressure above and higher pressure below, and that pressure difference lifts the plane while the pilot balances lift against weight and thrust against drag to steer.
